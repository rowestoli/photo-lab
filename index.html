Color from century-old glass plates

Between 1905 and 1915, Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire three times in a row, through blue, green and red filters, onto one glass plate. Stack the three negatives and the colors smear, because the camera and the scene moved between exposures. The aligner finds each channel's shift with a coarse-to-fine search.

HDR from a handheld bracket

I shot six exposures of a hallway on my phone, from 1/25 s to 1/10,000 s, without a tripod. No single frame holds both the ceiling lights and the shadows. The lab lines the frames up, recovers the camera's response curve, merges them into one radiance map and tone-maps it back down to a screen.

Recovered response curve

How the phone maps light to pixel values, solved per color channel from 200 sample pixels across all six exposures (Debevec and Malik, 1997). The bumps most likely come from the phone's own processing, which a camera in manual mode would not add.

Seamless blending

Pasting an object into a photo leaves a hard edge and the wrong lighting. Poisson blending keeps the object's texture (its gradients) and lets the new background decide its colors, by solving a sparse linear system over the masked pixels. Drag the object around; the system is solved again as you move it.

Panoramas and motion

These two projects lean on heavier machinery (SIFT features, GPU shaders), so they are shown as results from the course pipeline rather than run live.

Stitched panorama

SIFT features matched between neighboring shots, a homography fitted with RANSAC to throw out bad matches, then each photo warped into one frame. The wide shot below uses cylindrical projection instead, which keeps the edges from stretching.

Slow motion from optical flow

Pyramidal Lucas-Kanade estimates where every pixel moves between two frames. Warping both frames halfway along that flow and blending them synthesizes the frame in between, which turns ordinary video into smooth slow motion.

Adding damping to each Lucas-Kanade solve keeps the flow from blowing up in flat, textureless areas.
